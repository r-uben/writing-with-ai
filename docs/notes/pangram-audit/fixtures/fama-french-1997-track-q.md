## Draft: Fama & French (1997) — industry costs of equity

### Outline (confirmed)
1. Textbooks stress cash-flow risk; discount-rate uncertainty is understated.
2. CAPM vs FF3 both fit poorly as decision tools at industry granularity.
3. Factor-premium uncertainty + loading imprecision stack.
4. Firm/project estimates are worse; NPV precision is partly illusory.

### Draft

Corporate finance texts train managers to worry about forecast error in cash flows. They say less about forecast error in the discount rate — yet the rate is itself an estimate, and for industries it is a noisy one. Fama and French show that standard errors above 3.0% per year are typical when you apply either the CAPM or their three-factor model to industry portfolios. A "cost of equity" quoted to one decimal place misstates what the data allow.

Two errors compound. First, factor risk premia are inferred from long historical samples and remain uncertain even after decades of data. Second, industry loadings on those factors are estimated with error — sometimes substantial error — because industries are coarse buckets and betas shift with composition. Model choice does not rescue you. The CAPM is the textbook default but fares poorly as a description of the cross-section; the three-factor model fits better in sample yet is empirically motivated rather than derived from a tight utility foundation. Under either specification, the industry-level cost of equity is measured with bands wide enough to swamp many projects' assumed spreads.

If industries are this imprecise, single-firm and project-level estimates — built from shorter histories and noisier regressions — are worse. Precision in NPV is partly illusory unless estimation risk enters the decision explicitly.
