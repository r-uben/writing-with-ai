# Concept: Morris & Shin (1998) — unique equilibrium in currency attacks

**Source paper:** `1998__morris_shin__unique_equilibrium_self_fulfilling_currency_attacks__AER`
**Register:** theoretical macro / global games
**Target:** ~220 words, theory prose

## Central claim

Self-fulfilling currency attacks are usually modeled with multiple equilibria under common knowledge of fundamentals; adding a small amount of private noise to speculators' signals breaks common knowledge and yields a unique threshold equilibrium — which finally lets you analyze policies like transaction-cost "sand in the wheels."

## Confirmed outline

1. Multiple-equilibrium models of speculative attacks explain why crises feel arbitrary but cannot say when attacks occur or evaluate policy — any outcome can be rationalized.
2. The missing ingredient is not more fundamentals but less common knowledge: each speculator sees fundamentals with noise, so "everyone knows fundamentals are sound" ≠ "everyone knows everyone knows."
3. With noisy private signals, attack is a coordination game with a unique cutoff state θ*: below it, attacking dominates; above it, holding dominates — timing and policy become comparative-statics questions.
4. Implication: financial variables (mass of hot money, attack cost t) shift the cutoff; "throwing sand" in the wheels is not hand-waving — it moves the unique equilibrium. Transparency of monetary policy matters because it changes higher-order beliefs, not just first-order information.

## Supplied facts (verify before use)

- Model: continuum of speculators, peg at e*, attack cost t > 0, fundamentals θ ∈ [0,1]
- Classic references in paper: Obstfeld, Flood-Garber, Calvo debt crises
- Policy debate hook: Eichengreen et al. (1995) on capital mobility and sand-in-the-wheels
