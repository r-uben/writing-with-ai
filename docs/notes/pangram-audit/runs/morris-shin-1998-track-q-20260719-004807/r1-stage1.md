Call a currency crisis self-fulfilling: speculators expect an attack, their sales force the devaluation, and the expectation proves itself. Common-knowledge models of fundamentals reproduce that story — along with multiple equilibria. Why did the ERM break in September 1992 rather than September 1990? These models stay silent. Nor can they score a policy that raises the cost of short-selling. After the fact, any outcome fits.

The fix runs the other way. Not richer fundamentals — poorer common knowledge. Hand each speculator a private, noisy signal about θ. Now everyone can know the economy is sound yet not know that everyone else knows it, and not know that everyone knows that everyone knows it. Coordination rides on beliefs about others' beliefs, not on θ alone.

That structure kills the multiplicity. One cutoff survives, θ*: below it, attacking dominates; above it, holding dominates. Hot money in circulation, the attack cost t, the government's defense cost c(α, θ) — each enters as a comparative static. Throwing sand in the wheels stops being a metaphor once you leave the model; it moves the unique threshold. Monetary-policy transparency works for the same reason. It shifts higher-order beliefs, not just the first-order forecast of θ.

Multiple equilibria left policy with nothing to say. A unique global-games cutoff makes timing and intervention analysable.
