## Draft: Morris & Shin (1998) — unique equilibrium in currency attacks

### Outline (confirmed)
1. Multiple-equilibrium attack models explain self-fulfillment but not timing or policy.
2. Noisy private signals break common knowledge of fundamentals.
3. Unique threshold θ*: attack below, hold above.
4. Financial variables and sand-in-the-wheels shift the cutoff; transparency matters.

### Draft

Currency crises are often described as self-fulfilling: if speculators expect an attack, their sales force the devaluation that justifies the expectation. Models with common knowledge of fundamentals deliver that story — and multiple equilibria. They cannot tell you why the ERM broke in September 1992 rather than September 1990, and they cannot evaluate a policy that raises the cost of short-selling. Any outcome can be rationalized after the fact.

The fix is not richer fundamentals; it is poorer common knowledge. Give each speculator a private noisy signal about θ. Everyone may know the economy is sound, yet not know that everyone else knows it — and not know that everyone knows that everyone knows it. Coordination then turns on beliefs about others' beliefs, not θ alone.

With that information structure the multiplicity disappears. There is a unique cutoff θ*: below it, attacking dominates; above it, holding dominates. Hot money in circulation, attack cost t, and the government's defense cost c(α, θ) enter as comparative statics — "throwing sand in the wheels" is not a metaphor outside the model; it shifts the unique threshold. Transparency of monetary policy matters for the same reason: it changes higher-order beliefs, not just the first-order forecast of θ.

Multiple equilibria were a counsel of despair for policy. A unique global-games cutoff is what makes timing and intervention analysable.
