## Draft (~305 words)

On March 20, 2001, the FOMC surprised markets with a larger-than-expected 50-basis-point funds-rate cut. Within thirty minutes the S&P 500 fell. Jarociński and Karadi (2020) note that about one-third of FOMC announcements since 1990 produce the same positive co-movement of interest rates and stock prices. The episode is awkward for any reading that treats the announcement surprise as a pure policy innovation.

Their fix is a sign restriction on that co-movement in a half-hour window. A pure policy tightening should raise rates and lower stocks: discount rates rise and expected dividends fall. Negative co-movement of three-month fed-funds futures and the S&P 500 therefore marks a monetary policy shock; positive co-movement marks a central-bank information shock. In a Bayesian SVAR that embeds both high-frequency surprises, the purged policy shock looks like a textbook contraction—including a clearer decline in the price level—while an information shock that raises rates also raises activity and prices. Ignoring the second object biases inference on monetary nonneutrality.

Caballero and Simsek (2022) supply a complementary theory of why belief conflict is first-order for policy. The Fed and markets often disagree about the path of rates—Greenbook assumptions and FOMC dots versus forwards—partly because they disagree about future activity. In their model, opinionated demand disagreements lead the market to anticipate Fed “mistakes,” which move current demand and induce the Fed to partially accommodate the market’s view and to implement its own view only gradually. Announcements that reveal an unexpected change in the Fed’s belief microfound textbook-looking policy shocks. Tantrum shocks arise when the market misreads that belief and overreacts. Uncertainty about tantrums motivates further gradualism and communication. Disagreement also shifts market expected inflation and creates a cost-push-like policy trade-off.

Read together, the announcement window is a belief event as much as a rate event. Empirically, failing to purge information shocks biases nonneutrality. Theoretically, disagreement and misinterpretation are why optimal policy under the Fed’s own belief still looks gradual and communicative.

This note synthesizes the two mechanisms. It does not re-estimate either design or decide which belief channel dominates in any subsample.
