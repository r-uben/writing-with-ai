## Central claim (owned)

High-frequency “monetary policy surprises” are not pure policy innovations: they mix the central bank’s rate decision with news about beliefs—either the Fed’s private outlook (Jarociński–Karadi) or Fed–market disagreement about demand (Caballero–Simsek). Treating the composite as a textbook policy shock biases inference on nonneutrality and misreads why gradualism and communication matter.

## Confirmed outline (one point per paragraph)

1. **Concrete opener:** On March 20, 2001, the FOMC cut the funds rate by a larger-than-expected 50 bp, yet the S&P 500 fell within 30 minutes; about one-third of FOMC announcements since 1990 show this same positive co-movement of rates and stocks (Jarociński–Karadi). That pattern is the empirical foothold.
2. **JK mechanism:** Pure policy tightening should raise rates and lower stocks (higher discount rates + weaker expected dividends). Negative rate–stock co-movement in a half-hour window around FOMC is therefore the policy-shock signature; positive co-movement identifies a central-bank *information* shock. A Bayesian SVAR with those high-frequency sign restrictions separates the two; purged policy shocks look more like textbook contractions (including a clearer price-level decline), while information shocks raise rates *and* activity/prices.
3. **CS mechanism:** Independently, the Fed and markets often disagree about the path of rates (Greenbook / dots vs forwards), partly reflecting different views of future activity. In Caballero–Simsek’s model, opinionated demand disagreements make the market anticipate Fed “mistakes,” which move current demand and induce the Fed to *partially accommodate* the market and implement its own view only gradually. Announcements that reveal an unexpected change in the Fed’s belief microfound textbook-looking policy shocks; *tantrum* shocks arise when the market misreads the Fed’s belief and overreacts—motivating further gradualism and communication. Disagreement also warps market expected inflation and creates a cost-push-like trade-off.
4. **Synthesis punchline:** Read together, both papers say the object in the announcement window is a *belief event* as much as a rate event. Empirically (JK), failing to purge information shocks biases nonneutrality. Theoretically (CS), disagreement and misinterpretation are why policy must be gradual and communicative even when the Fed is optimizing under its own belief.
5. **Scope fence:** This note synthesizes identification and theory mechanisms from the two papers; it does not re-estimate either design or adjudicate which belief channel dominates in any subsample.
