### Draft

On March 20, 2001, the FOMC cut the federal funds rate by a larger-than-expected 50 basis points, and within thirty minutes the S&P 500 declined. Jarociński and Karadi (2020) report that about one-third of FOMC announcements since 1990 exhibit the same positive co-movement between interest rates and stock prices. That is hard to reconcile with a reading in which the announcement surprise is only an unexpected shift in the policy instrument.

High-frequency identification around FOMC releases uses the co-movement of three-month fed funds futures and the S&P 500 in a half-hour window. Under a broad class of models, a policy tightening that is not accompanied by other news raises rates and lowers equity values, because discount rates rise and expected dividends fall. Negative co-movement of rates and stocks in that window is therefore used to isolate a monetary policy shock; positive co-movement is used to isolate a central-bank information shock—news about the central bank’s assessment of the outlook rather than about the stance alone. Jarociński and Karadi impose those sign restrictions in a Bayesian SVAR. After the information component is removed, the remaining policy shock produces contractions in output and tighter financial conditions, and a clearer decline in the price level, closer to what standard monetary models predict. If the information component is left inside a single “policy surprise,” estimates of nonneutrality inherit both shocks.

Caballero and Simsek (2022) address a related belief conflict from the other direction. Greenbook assumptions and FOMC dots often diverge from market forwards, in part because the Fed and the market disagree about future activity. In their model, that disagreement leads the market to anticipate policy the Fed would regard as mistaken; current demand responds to that anticipation; and the Fed optimally accommodates the market’s view in part while implementing its own view only as beliefs update. An announcement that unexpectedly revises the Fed’s belief then moves markets much as a conventional policy shock would. When the market instead misreads the Fed’s belief and overreacts, the same announcement becomes a tantrum. Gradualism and communication limit exposure to those misreadings. Disagreement also shifts market expected inflation and introduces a trade-off similar to a cost-push shock.

Taken together, the high-frequency surprise is a change in the policy rate bundled with a revision in beliefs—either the central bank’s private assessment of demand (Jarociński–Karadi) or the gap between Fed and market views of demand (Caballero–Simsek). Treating the bundle as a pure policy innovation biases inference on nonneutrality and obscures why an optimizing Fed may still move gradually and invest in communication.

This note only synthesizes those two mechanisms. It does not re-estimate either empirical design, and it does not claim which belief channel dominates in a given sample.
